The SNAP-IV is the workhorse pediatric ADHD rating scale. Twenty-six
items rated by a parent (or teacher, with different cutoffs), covering
the three diagnostic clusters that come up over and over in
6-18-year-olds: inattention, hyperactivity/impulsivity, and oppositional
behaviour.

## What it measures

Three subscales mapped directly to DSM-IV criteria:

- **Inattention** (items 1-9) — careless mistakes, sustained attention,
  following through, organisation, losing things, distractibility,
  forgetfulness
- **Hyperactivity/Impulsivity** (items 10-18) — fidgeting, leaving seat,
  excessive talking, interrupting, can't wait turn, "on the go"
- **Oppositional** (items 19-26) — temper, arguing with adults, defying
  rules, deliberately annoying others, blaming others

The combination lets you see at a glance whether you're looking at
ADHD, ODD, or — often — both.

## When to use it

- Initial pediatric ADHD/ODD evaluation
- Treatment-response tracking, especially when medication or behavioural
  intervention starts
- Cross-informant comparison — give the parent version, then a separate
  copy to the teacher, and compare subscale patterns

## How parents fill it out

Five to ten minutes. Each item rates a behaviour over the past six
months (or since the school year began) on a 0-3 scale (Not at all /
Just a little / Quite a bit / Very much).

## Scoring and severity cutoffs

Subscale sums with **parent-rated cutoffs** derived from Swanson's
canonical average thresholds × item count.

| Subscale | Items | Parent cutoff | Per-item average |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inattention | 1-9 | ≥16 | ≥1.78 |
| Hyperactivity/Impulsivity | 10-18 | ≥13 | ≥1.44 |
| Oppositional | 19-26 | ≥15 | ≥1.88 |

### Teacher cutoffs are higher

If you administer this same form to a teacher instead of a parent,
apply these higher sum thresholds when you read the result:

- Inattention ≥23
- Hyperactivity/Impulsivity ≥16
- Oppositional ≥16

Same instrument, same wording — the population difference (teachers
observe a comparison group of classroom peers) is what raises the
threshold. A teacher-specific variant of the template isn't in the
library yet; for now, capture the teacher's responses on the parent
form and apply the higher cutoffs by hand.

## SNAP-IV vs ASRS, and vs the Conners

Age decides the first one. The SNAP-IV is the parent- or teacher-rated
scale for children and adolescents; the
[ASRS v1.1](/clinical-templates/child/asrs-v1-1) is the adult self-report
scale. The SNAP-IV also covers oppositional symptoms, which the ASRS does
not — useful, because ADHD and oppositional presentations arrive together
often enough that measuring only one gives you half the picture.

For comorbidity, pair it with the
[RCADS](/clinical-templates/child/rcads-parent) for anxiety and
depression, or the [SDQ](/clinical-templates/child/sdq-self) for a broader
screen across conduct and peer relationships.

Against the Conners scales: the Conners is the other widely used pediatric
ADHD rating scale, and it is licensed by MHS and paid for per use. Rivet
doesn't include it. The SNAP-IV covers the same three domains — inattention,
hyperactivity/impulsivity and oppositional symptoms — is public domain, and
carries no fee for however many families you use it with. If a school board
or assessment protocol requires the Conners specifically, you'll be running
it wherever you licensed it.

## Is it free to use?

Yes. The SNAP-IV is public domain — there is no licence to buy, no permission to
request and no fee, whether you use it once or with every family on your
caseload. That holds for the parent-rated version here and for the teacher-rated
version you may be sent alongside it.

That is unusual enough to be worth knowing. Several of the better-known
pediatric ADHD scales are copyrighted and priced per administration, which is
often what decides whether a practice uses them at all.
